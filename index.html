Problem Space

The campus shuttle service is an important mode of transportation for many students, especially day boarders who depend on it to travel between the campus and nearby transport points. During daily commuting, students often experience delays, uncertainty, and difficulty obtaining reliable information about the shuttle service. These issues affect their punctuality, daily planning, and overall commuting experience.

Note on process: at this stage no app, feature, or solution has been proposed. The problem is physical and experiential — the research below exists to understand it before deciding whether, or how, to respond to it.

Exhibit B · Research-based

Observation Log

Three students were present at the campus shuttle stop. Two were male day-boarders and one was a female hostel student. They arrived at 2:13 PM, two minutes before the scheduled shuttle arrival time of 2:15 PM, and waited near the stop.

The shuttle did not arrive at the scheduled time. After a few minutes, the two male students approached the security guard to ask about the shuttle and then returned to the waiting area. During this time, the female student checked her phone several times while waiting.

After waiting for around 20 minutes, the female student asked, “Does this bus always get late?” One of the male students replied, “Yeah, it's an all-time problem.” The students continued waiting until the shuttle arrived at approximately 2:45 PM, around 30 minutes later than its scheduled arrival time. They immediately boarded the shuttle, and it departed shortly afterwards.

Objective record — no interpretation applied here

Reflection

Conclusion

Field observation of three real students revealed that the campus shuttle's core problem was not a lack of technology, but a lack of reliable information during unpredictable waits. The empathy map and journey map located the lowest emotional point precisely at the "Wait" and "Ask" stages — the moments with no information at all. That single pain point produced three user stories, which in turn produced twenty candidate content items. Card sorting with eleven students and a tree test with five students validated a four-category information structure without requiring any changes. MoSCoW and a DFV matrix then narrowed twenty items down to four evidence-backed must-haves, with Schedule Change Alerts winning the closest contest because it addresses the uncertainty — not just the delay itself. The research shaped every decision in this document; no solution was assumed before the users were understood.

Peer 1 BENCHMARK RECORD

Administrative Request Tracking System

DOMAIN / PROBLEM

Reducing uncertainty after students submit administrative requests at Vijaybhoomi University.

INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE

Four groups: My Requests, New Request, Updates and Actions, and Help. Card sorting created the groups; tree testing then led to moving Delay Update into My Requests.

MUST-HAVE DECISION

They used MoSCoW, then DFV to compare Expected Completion Date and Progress Timeline. Expected Completion Date became the fourth Must-Have.

MAIN USER TASK

Track an administrative request: see current status, expected completion, and updates without repeated follow-ups.

ERROR / ALTERNATIVE STATES

The case study includes Pending Action, Missing Document Alert, Status Update Notification, Comments/Admin Updates, Help/FAQ and Contact Concerned Department. It does not document a specific prototype error-state flow.

KEY DIFFERENCE FROM MY PROJECT

The peer's system focuses on administrative-request tracking and groups progress/time information under My Requests. My project focuses on shuttle information and separates Status, Arrival, Delay and Schedule Change.

WHY THIS APPROACH

Tree testing showed that users expected progress and time-related information together, so Delay Update was moved into My Requests.

WHAT I LEARNED

IA should follow users' mental models and be changed when testing reveals navigation confusion.

Peer 2 BENCHMARK RECORD

RouteMate

DOMAIN / PROBLEM

Students who commute from home to college face delays and cancellations in public or college transport, making it difficult to find a reliable backup option and causing late arrival to lectures.

INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE

The IA is organized around the commuter’s journey. It starts with Home, then moves through My Commute and Live Status, followed by transport selection at Karjat. Users can compare options, confirm their choice, and track the active journey. Backup Options and SOS provide support, while alerts and alternatives handle delays or cancellations.

MUST-HAVE DECISION

Based on the commuter’s main pain point, focusing on live status, backup options, and handling delays/cancellations.

MAIN USER TASK

Plan and manage the daily commute to college, check live transport status, choose a suitable last-leg transport option, and switch to a saved backup plan when a delay or cancellation occurs.

ERROR / ALTERNATIVE STATES

It provides a Loading state while live data is being fetched, an Error state with “Try Again,” “Use Last Status,” and “Back to Home” options, and a Travel Alert when the selected transport is cancelled. Users can then choose an alternative or use their saved backup plan.

KEY DIFFERENCE FROM MY PROJECT

The main difference is that RouteMate focuses on commuter travel reliability and backup planning during delays or cancellations, while my project has a different primary problem and user flow. RouteMate specifically uses Live Status to trigger alternative transport and a Saved Backup Travel Plan.

WHY THIS APPROACH

They chose this approach because the main problem is unreliable transport during delays and cancellations. The approach provides live status, alternative transport options, and a saved backup plan so the commuter can continue the journey instead of being left without a dependable option.

WHAT I LEARNED

I learned that live-status information should be clear and up to date, and users should always get a chance to review changes before confirming them. Error, loading, and alternative states are also important for helping users recover when something goes wrong.

Peer 3 BENCHMARK RECORD

SmartSeat — Dining Hall Smart Seat Reservation System

DOMAIN / PROBLEM

Students have no way to know which dining-hall seats are free or claim one in advance, leading to wasted trips and crowding at peak lunch time.

INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE

Four main areas: Home/Availability (seat count + booking status), Seat Selection (row → table → seat), Check-in/Timing (countdown, check-in action), and Confirmation. Admin has a separate, structurally distinct dashboard (session control, seat/QR management, analytics).

MUST-HAVE DECISION

Driven by the physical constraints given (24 seats, one lunch window) and by what makes double-booking and no-shows impossible. Real-time sync, server-authoritative timing, and check-in were treated as non-negotiable Must-Haves; the visual floor map and multi-hall support were left as Won't-Have-for-now.

MAIN USER TASK

Reserve an available seat, check in within the grace window, and use it for a bounded duration before it is released back to the pool.

ERROR / ALTERNATIVE STATES

Explicit states cover booking-not-open, seat-just-taken conflicts, expired/no-show reservations, invalid or wrong-seat QR scans, and connection loss (with polling fallback). Error messages explain what happened and what to do next, such as choosing another seat after a conflict.

KEY DIFFERENCE FROM MY PROJECT

SmartSeat focuses on dining-hall seat reservation and uses real-time availability, check-in, and timed seat release. My project focuses on campus shuttle information, including current status, expected arrival time, delays, and schedule changes.

WHY THIS APPROACH

Because a physical seat can only be occupied by one person at a time and the exhibition demo depends on trust, double-booking prevention and visible system status (open/closed and time remaining) were prioritized over visual polish or extra features.

WHAT I LEARNED

Server-authoritative time and locking matter more than UI polish when multiple users interact with the same resource at the same time. Most of the important design decisions were about state and timing, not just layout.

FINAL INSIGHTS FROM THE SHEET Cross-peer synthesis
01 · SHARED PATTERN

All three peer projects organize information around the user's main task/problem. Each uses clear functional groups rather than organizing only by internal system features. Testing or real-world constraints influenced the structure.

02 · WHY THEY DIFFER

The peers differed because their core problems and constraints were different: administrative request progress, commuter reliability and backup travel, and dining-hall seat reservation with real-time concurrency. My Campus Shuttle project focuses on quickly finding current status, expected arrival, delay information, and schedule changes.

03 · WHAT I LEARNED

Peer 1 groups progress/time information under My Requests after tree-testing revealed a mental-model mismatch. Peer 2 uses Live Status to connect delays/cancellations to alternative transport and a saved backup plan. Peer 3 emphasizes server-authoritative timing, locking, check-in and explicit conflict/error states because seats are a shared resource. My IA separates shuttle status, arrival, delay and schedule information as distinct user-facing needs.

04 · NEXT ITERATION OPPORTUNITY

I learned that IA should follow the user's mental model and be validated through testing. I also learned that live information needs clear system status and recovery paths, and that alternative/error states should be designed as part of the main experience rather than treated as an afterthought. System constraints can also strongly affect IA and interaction logic.

Final reflection

One Continuous UX Process

The second half of the project did not replace the research that came before it. It carried the Final MVP forward: the research and synthesis established the IA; prioritization defined the four must-haves; wireframing translated them into screens; heuristic inspection exposed two documented usability issues and produced corresponding fixes; the prototype connected the screens and states; usability testing revealed remaining label confusion; and the next iteration identifies clearer descriptions as a proposed response. The project therefore ends with a tested direction, not a claim that every problem has been solved.
